    Ants are among the most successful and diverse insects on Earth, with over 12,000 known species. They belong to the family Formicidae and are known for their highly organised social structures, complex communication systems, and remarkable ecological roles. They are found in nearly every habitat, from tropical rainforests to deserts and the fringes of the polar regions.

    Despite their size, ants exhibit extraordinary problem-solving and cooperative behaviour. A colony can consist of thousands of individuals, each with a distinct role that contributes to the survival of the whole. What follows is an overview of their biology, their societies, and their significance in nature.

    Anatomy

    An ant's body is built from three segments, each carrying a different part of the workload.

    Body segments

    Ants have a three-segment body: the head, thorax and abdomen. The head contains the eyes, antennae and mouthparts, while the thorax supports the legs. The abdomen houses vital organs, including the digestive and reproductive systems.

    Antennae

    The antennae are highly sensitive and central to both communication and navigation. They detect chemical signals — pheromones — as well as changes in the surrounding environment, allowing ants to navigate complex terrain.

    Mouthparts

    Ants have specialised mouthparts adapted to different feeding strategies. Workers may have mandibles for cutting food, while queens have elongated mouthparts suited to egg-laying. Larvae and pupae lack mandibles altogether and rely on regurgitated food.

    Ecology and habitat

    Ants thrive across a wide range of environments, from dense forest to arid desert. They are particularly abundant in tropical regions, where biodiversity is high, and are found in soil, leaf litter, tree bark, and even within other organisms such as plants and animals.

    Tropical rainforests

    Rainforests provide ample food and shelter, making them ideal for many species. Leafcutter ants in particular play a critical role in nutrient cycling by breaking down plant material.

    Deserts

    Desert-dwelling ants, such as harvester ants, have adapted to extreme conditions. They store water and food in underground nests to survive long dry periods, and some species tolerate remarkably high temperatures.

    Grasslands and savannas

    Grasslands provide a mix of food sources and open space for foraging. Ants here interact heavily with other insects and with plants, contributing to the overall health of the ecosystem.

    Social structure

    Ants live in highly organised colonies. Each colony consists of three main castes: queens, workers, and males — sometimes called drones. The queen is responsible for reproduction, while workers forage, care for the young, and maintain the nest.

    Queens

    The queen is the reproductive female of the colony. She lays all the eggs and, in some species, can live for many years and produce thousands of offspring. Queens are typically larger than workers.

    Workers

    Workers are sterile females responsible for the colony's daily operation — foraging, tending larvae, and maintaining the nest. This division of labour is what keeps the colony viable.

    Males

    Males, or drones, are typically present only during the mating season. Their role is to mate with queens from other colonies; after mating they usually die, so their presence in the colony is brief.

    Communication

    Ants communicate through a combination of chemical signals and physical interaction. Pheromones are secreted to mark trails, attract mates and signal danger, and both the presence and the concentration of a pheromone influence how other ants behave.

    Pheromone trails

    When an ant finds food it lays a pheromone trail that guides other ants back to the source. This lets a colony locate and exploit food efficiently, cutting the time and energy spent searching.

    Tactile communication

    Ants also communicate by touch, using their antennae to detect vibration and chemical signals from other ants, which lets them coordinate movement and respond to changes around them.

    Visual signals

    Some species use body posture and movement to convey information — warning others of a predator, for instance, or signalling that food has been found.

    Ecological role

    Ants are often described as ecosystem engineers, because they modify their environment in ways that create habitat for other species. Leafcutter ants, for example, cultivate fungus gardens inside their nests that support a diverse community of microorganisms.

    Nutrient cycling

    Ants break down organic matter such as dead plants and animals, returning nutrients to the soil. The process is essential to plant growth and to the health of the ecosystem overall.

    Pollination

    Less famously than bees, some ant species collect nectar and pollen, inadvertently transferring pollen between flowers and aiding plant reproduction.

    Pest control

    Ants keep other insect populations in check by preying on them, and compete with termites and other soil-dwelling insects, helping to maintain balance.

    Human interaction

    Human relationships with ants run both ways. In agriculture they can help control pests; elsewhere, certain species damage crops, structures and stored food.

    Beneficial species

    Carpenter ants help decompose wood and recycle nutrients in natural ecosystems. In agriculture, harvester ants can be useful in controlling pest populations.

    Problematic species

    Fire ants and Argentine ants are widely considered pests. They damage crops, contaminate food, and can cause allergic reactions, so management is often necessary.

    Cultural significance

    Ants appear throughout art, literature and folklore as symbols of hard work, cooperation and resilience. Several cultures single them out specifically for their intelligence and organisation.

    Conservation

    Ants are generally resilient and adaptable, but some species face pressure from habitat loss, climate change and invasive competitors. Conservation work concentrates on three fronts.

    Habitat protection

    Conserving forests and grasslands keeps ant populations in contact with the resources they depend on, and reduces the risk of local extinction.

    Reducing pesticide use

    Heavy pesticide use harms ant populations directly and disrupts the ecosystems they belong to. Sustainable farming practices protect ants alongside other beneficial insects.

    Research and education

    Further research into ant biology and ecology helps identify conservation priorities, and public understanding of what ants do tends to translate into support for protecting them.
